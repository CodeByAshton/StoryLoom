The rain had been at it since noon, patient as a debt collector. Mara stood under the dripping eaves of Blackwood Manor and counted the lit windows. Seven. A house that burned that much oil was either very rich or very frightened.

The door opened before she could knock. Arthur Vane filled it, all good tailoring and bad sleep.

"My dear detective," he said. "You came."

"I came for the fee, Vane. Let's not make it romantic."

He laughed as if she had been charming and led her through a hall of portraits that all had his chin. The servants had gone to bed, or had been sent there. Their footsteps were the only sound in the house besides the rain.

The study was on the second floor. Someone had been thorough with it. Drawers lay upside down on the carpet. The curtains had been slit to the rod. Every book had been taken down and shaken, and the shelves gaped like a mouth with its teeth knocked out.

The safe stood open in the corner. Mara crouched in front of it.

"No scratches," she said. "No powder. Whoever opened this had the key."

"Impossible. There is only one key, and I keep it on my person."

"Then you opened it." She stood. "Or someone borrowed your person."

Arthur did not laugh this time. He poured two glasses of something amber and handed her one she did not drink.

"The ledger is an old book of household accounts," he said. "Sentimental value. My grandfather's hand."

"People don't slit curtains for sentiment."

"You are very direct."

"You are paying me to be." She set the glass on the desk. "Tell me what's really in it."

He looked at the window, where their two reflections hung over the dark garden. For a moment she thought he might tell her.

"Debts," he said at last. "Old ones. Nothing that concerns you."

Mara had heard that sentence before, from men who knew exactly how much it concerned her. Three years ago a Syndicate clerk had shown her a page from a black book with her name on it, and she had been dismissed from the force a week later. She had never learned what she owed, or to whom.

"I'll find your ledger," she said. "And when I do, I'll read it."

"I would expect nothing less." Arthur raised his glass to her. "To our partnership."

She didn't raise hers. Outside, the rain kept counting.
